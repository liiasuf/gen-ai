IELTS Academic: Building Academic Vocabulary and Paraphrasing Skills

A wide and accurate vocabulary is one of the most important factors in achieving a high score across all four sections of IELTS Academic, but particularly in Reading, Writing, and Speaking, where Lexical Resource is explicitly named as one of the assessment criteria. Academic vocabulary differs from everyday conversational vocabulary in that it includes more formal words and phrases, precise terms for describing data and trends, and vocabulary related to commonly discussed academic topics such as the environment, education, technology, health, urbanization, globalization, and economics.

One of the most valuable skills for IELTS candidates is paraphrasing — the ability to express the same idea using different words and structures. Paraphrasing is important for several reasons. In the Reading section, many questions rephrase information from the passage using synonyms, so recognizing that a question's wording corresponds to a different wording in the text is essential for finding the correct answer. In the Writing section, both tasks require candidates to paraphrase the question in their introduction, and avoiding repetition of the same words throughout an essay is rewarded under Lexical Resource. In the Speaking section, paraphrasing helps candidates avoid awkward repetition and demonstrates a wider vocabulary range to the examiner.

There are several common techniques for paraphrasing effectively. The first is using synonyms — replacing a word with another word of similar meaning, such as replacing "important" with "significant," "crucial," or "essential," depending on context. The second is changing word forms — for example, turning the verb "increase" into the noun "an increase" or the adjective "increasing," which allows a sentence to be restructured while keeping a similar meaning. The third is changing sentence structure — for example, turning an active sentence such as "Many students use the library every day" into a passive sentence such as "The library is used by many students every day," or combining two short sentences into one complex sentence using a subordinating conjunction.

A fourth technique is changing the order of information within a sentence, particularly useful when paraphrasing a Task 1 or Task 2 question in an essay introduction — for example, a question that begins "Some people believe that..." could be paraphrased as "It is often argued that...," followed by a restatement of the rest of the idea in different words. Candidates should be careful, however, not to change the meaning of the original statement when paraphrasing; a paraphrase should preserve the same essential information while using different vocabulary or grammar.

Common collocations — words that frequently occur together — are also valuable for sounding natural and for expressing ideas precisely. Examples relevant to academic topics include "address an issue," "raise awareness," "have a significant impact on," "play a vital role in," "strike a balance between," and "draw a conclusion." Using collocations correctly is often noticed positively by examiners as a sign of a wide and natural vocabulary range, whereas overly literal or word-for-word translations from another language can sound unnatural even if technically grammatical.

For describing data in Writing Task 1, a specific set of vocabulary is especially useful: verbs of change such as "rise," "increase," "grow," "climb," "decline," "decrease," "fall," "drop," "fluctuate," and "remain stable" or "level off"; adverbs of degree such as "slightly," "moderately," "significantly," "substantially," and "dramatically"; and nouns such as "an upward trend," "a downward trend," "a peak," "a low point," and "a plateau."

Building this kind of vocabulary is best done through wide reading and listening — for example, reading newspaper articles or academic texts on a range of topics, and noting down useful words, phrases, and collocations in context, rather than memorizing long isolated word lists. Reviewing and actively using new vocabulary in practice essays, speaking practice, and summaries helps move words from passive recognition into active, usable vocabulary that a candidate can draw on naturally during the test.
