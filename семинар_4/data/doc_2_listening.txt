IELTS Academic: The Listening Section

The Listening section is the same for both the Academic and General Training versions of IELTS. It consists of four recorded sections, played once only, and a total of 40 questions. Candidates have 30 minutes to listen to the recordings and answer the questions, plus an extra 10 minutes at the end of the paper-based test to transfer their answers onto the official answer sheet. In the computer-delivered version, answers are typed directly during the test and there is no separate transfer time.

The four sections of the Listening test increase in difficulty as the test progresses, and each section focuses on a different type of listening situation. Section 1 is typically a conversation between two people in an everyday social context, such as booking accommodation, making travel arrangements, or asking for information about a service. Section 2 is usually a monologue set in an everyday social context, for example a speech about local facilities or an announcement about an event. Section 3 is a conversation involving up to four people in an educational or training context, such as a discussion between students and a tutor about an assignment. Section 4 is a monologue on an academic subject, such as a university lecture.

The 40 questions in the Listening test are drawn from a range of question types, including: multiple choice, matching, plan/map/diagram labeling, form completion, note completion, table completion, flow-chart completion, summary completion, sentence completion, and short-answer questions. Each correct answer is worth one mark, and the total raw score out of 40 is converted to a band score from 0 to 9 using a standard conversion table published by IELTS.

A common strategy for the Listening test is to read the questions before the recording begins, since each section typically provides a short pause to allow candidates to look ahead. This helps candidates predict the type of information they need to listen for — for example, a name, a number, a date, or a specific word from a limited set of options. Candidates should pay close attention to instructions about word limits, such as "Write NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER," because answers that exceed the stated limit are marked incorrect even if the content is correct.

Another important skill is recognizing "distractors" — pieces of information in the recording that initially seem like they could be the answer but are later corrected or rejected by the speaker. For example, a speaker might say "the meeting is on Tuesday... actually, no, let's make it Wednesday," and only "Wednesday" would be the correct answer. Listening for signal words such as "actually," "but," "however," and "instead" can help candidates avoid being misled by distractors.

Spelling accuracy matters in the Listening test: answers must be spelled correctly to be marked correct, including proper nouns such as names of people and places, which are often spelled out loud by one of the speakers. Numbers, dates, and times should be written in the format that matches standard English conventions unless otherwise specified.

Because the recording is played only once, with no opportunity to replay any part of it, time management within each section is critical. Candidates who miss an answer should not dwell on it, but should move on immediately to avoid losing track of the next question. At the end of the test, in the paper-based format, the 10 minutes of transfer time should be used carefully to copy answers accurately from the question booklet to the answer sheet, double-checking spelling and number formats as they go.

Common preparation activities for the Listening section include practicing with a wide variety of English accents (British, Australian, American, and others may all appear in the recordings), building familiarity with academic and everyday vocabulary, and doing full-length timed practice tests to build stamina and concentration for the entire 30-to-40-minute listening experience.
