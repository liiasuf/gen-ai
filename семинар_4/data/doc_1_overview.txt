IELTS Academic: Overview of the Test

The International English Language Testing System (IELTS) Academic module is designed for people who want to study at a university or other institution of higher education, or who are seeking professional registration in an English-speaking country. It is different from the IELTS General Training module, which is intended for people migrating to English-speaking countries for work, secondary education, or immigration purposes. While both modules share the same Listening and Speaking components, the Reading and Writing sections differ in content and task type.

The IELTS Academic test is made up of four sections, always taken in the same order: Listening, Reading, Writing, and Speaking. The Listening, Reading, and Writing sections are completed on the same day, with no breaks between them. The Speaking test may be scheduled on the same day or up to seven days before or after the other three sections, depending on the test center.

The total test time for Listening, Reading, and Writing is approximately two hours and forty minutes. The Listening section lasts 30 minutes, plus an additional 10 minutes for candidates to transfer their answers (in the paper-based version). The Reading section lasts 60 minutes, and the Writing section also lasts 60 minutes, split between two tasks: Task 1 (20 minutes recommended) and Task 2 (40 minutes recommended). The Speaking section lasts between 11 and 14 minutes and is conducted as a face-to-face interview with a certified examiner, or in some locations, as a video call.

Across the Listening and Reading sections combined, candidates answer a total of 80 questions: 40 questions in Listening and 40 questions in Reading. The Writing section does not have a fixed number of "questions" in the same sense, since it consists of two extended writing tasks that are assessed against detailed scoring criteria rather than marked right or wrong.

IELTS Academic can be taken in two formats: paper-based and computer-delivered. In the paper-based format, candidates write their answers on an answer sheet using a pencil. In the computer-delivered format, candidates type their answers using a keyboard, and the test interface displays a countdown timer for each section. The content, timing, and question types are the same in both formats, although the computer-delivered Listening section does not include the extra 10-minute transfer time, since answers are entered directly during the recording.

Each section of the test is scored on the IELTS 9-band scale, where Band 9 represents an expert user and Band 1 represents a non-user. The four section scores are then averaged to produce an Overall Band Score, which is also reported on the 9-band scale, in increments of 0.5 or whole bands. This Overall Band Score, along with the four individual section scores, is what universities, employers, and immigration authorities use to assess a candidate's English proficiency.

Most universities accepting international students set a minimum Overall Band Score requirement, commonly somewhere between 6.0 and 7.5, and many also specify minimum scores for individual sections (for example, requiring at least 6.0 in Writing even if the overall score is higher). This is because the four skills measured by IELTS — Listening, Reading, Writing, and Speaking — are considered independently important for academic success, and a strong score in one area cannot fully compensate for weak performance in another.

Test results are typically available 13 days after the test date for the paper-based format, and as little as 3 to 5 days for the computer-delivered format. Candidates receive a Test Report Form showing their scores for each of the four sections as well as their Overall Band Score. IELTS results are considered valid for two years from the test date by most receiving organizations, although this is a guideline rather than a strict rule set by IELTS itself.

Preparation for IELTS Academic typically involves becoming familiar with the format and timing of each section, practicing with sample materials under timed conditions, building academic vocabulary, and developing strategies specific to each task type. Because the test assesses general academic English ability rather than knowledge of a particular subject, candidates from any academic background can prepare effectively using general practice materials, sample tests, and structured study plans.
