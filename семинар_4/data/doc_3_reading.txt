IELTS Academic: The Reading Section

The Academic Reading section consists of three long passages, totaling 40 questions, to be completed within 60 minutes. Unlike the Listening section, there is no extra transfer time — all answers must be written directly onto the answer sheet (or typed, in the computer-delivered version) within the 60-minute limit. The passages are taken from books, journals, magazines, and newspapers, and are written for a non-specialist audience, meaning candidates do not need specialized subject knowledge to understand them. At least one passage typically contains a detailed logical argument, and texts may include diagrams, graphs, or illustrations.

The three passages generally increase in difficulty, although the order in which question types appear is not fixed. Common question types include: multiple choice, identifying information (True/False/Not Given), identifying the writer's views or claims (Yes/No/Not Given), matching information to paragraphs, matching headings to paragraphs, matching features, matching sentence endings, sentence completion, summary/note/table/flow-chart completion, diagram label completion, and short-answer questions.

The True/False/Not Given and Yes/No/Not Given question types are often considered among the trickiest for candidates. In these questions, "TRUE" or "YES" means the statement agrees with the information or claims in the passage; "FALSE" or "NO" means the statement contradicts the information or claims in the passage; and "NOT GIVEN" means there is no information about the statement in the passage at all — the passage neither confirms nor denies it. A frequent mistake is for candidates to mark "NOT GIVEN" when the answer is actually "FALSE" (because the passage says the opposite) or to mark "FALSE" when the topic simply is not discussed and the correct answer is "NOT GIVEN." Careful, literal reading of the passage — rather than reliance on general knowledge or assumptions — is essential for these question types.

Matching headings to paragraphs requires candidates to identify the main idea of each paragraph and match it to one heading from a list, which usually contains more headings than there are paragraphs. This question type tests overall comprehension rather than detail, so candidates are advised to read each paragraph's first and last sentences carefully, since topic sentences often appear there, before reading the whole paragraph if needed.

Two key reading strategies for the Academic Reading test are skimming and scanning. Skimming means reading quickly to get a general sense of a text's structure, main ideas, and overall argument, without reading every word. Scanning means searching quickly through a text for specific information, such as a date, a name, or a particular keyword, without reading for overall meaning. Skimming is useful for getting an initial overview of a passage before tackling questions about its structure or main ideas, while scanning is useful for locating the part of the passage relevant to a specific detail question.

Vocabulary in context is also frequently tested. Candidates may be asked to complete a summary using words from the passage, or to choose the best synonym for a highlighted word. Building a wide academic vocabulary, including common collocations and topic-specific terms related to subjects like the environment, education, technology, health, and history, can significantly improve performance, since many questions paraphrase the original wording of the passage rather than repeating it exactly.

Time management is one of the biggest challenges in the Reading test, since 60 minutes for three passages and 40 questions means an average of 20 minutes per passage, including reading and answering. Many candidates find it helpful to attempt the passage they find easiest first, in order to secure those marks before moving on to more challenging texts. As with Listening, each correct answer is worth one mark, and the raw score out of 40 is converted to a band score using a published conversion table.
