IELTS Academic: Sample Practice Questions and Explanations

This document provides a small set of sample practice questions, in the style of IELTS Academic Reading and Listening tasks, along with explanations of the correct answers and the reasoning behind them. These examples are intended to illustrate how question types work and how to approach common pitfalls, rather than to serve as an exhaustive practice set.

Sample 1 — True/False/Not Given (Reading style). Passage extract: "The university library, originally built in 1932, was extended twice during the twentieth century, most recently in 1987, and now houses over two million volumes." Statement: "The library has been extended on more than one occasion." The correct answer is TRUE, because the passage explicitly states the library "was extended twice," which directly supports the statement that it has been extended more than once. A candidate who answers FALSE or NOT GIVEN here has likely misread the word "twice" or failed to connect "extended twice" with "more than one occasion."

Sample 2 — True/False/Not Given (Reading style). Same passage extract as above. Statement: "The 1987 extension doubled the size of the library's collection." The correct answer is NOT GIVEN, because while the passage mentions the 1987 extension and the current size of the collection (over two million volumes), it does not state how large the collection was before 1987 or whether the extension specifically doubled it. A candidate who answers TRUE here may be making an unsupported inference, while a candidate who answers FALSE may be assuming the opposite without textual evidence — in both cases, the correct response is that the passage simply does not provide this information.

Sample 3 — Matching headings (Reading style). Paragraph extract: "Despite initial skepticism from local residents, the new recycling program achieved a 40% increase in household participation within its first year, attributed largely to a series of community workshops explaining how the new sorting system worked." Possible headings include: (A) "Early resistance overcome through education," (B) "A decline in environmental awareness," (C) "The history of recycling policy," (D) "Funding challenges for local programs." The correct heading is (A), because the paragraph describes initial skepticism (resistance) being overcome (resulting in increased participation) through community workshops (education). Heading (B) is incorrect because the paragraph describes an increase, not a decline, in engagement. Headings (C) and (D) are not addressed at all in this paragraph, illustrating how matching-headings distractors often relate to the general topic area without matching the paragraph's specific content.

Sample 4 — Multiple choice (Listening style). Audio transcript extract: "We were originally planning to hold the workshop in the main hall, but due to renovation work, it's been moved to the conference room on the second floor — that's room 204, not 240 as it says on the original flyer." Question: "Where will the workshop be held?" Options: (A) The main hall, (B) Room 240, (C) Room 204. The correct answer is (C) Room 204, because the speaker corrects two pieces of information: the location has changed from the main hall to the conference room, and the room number on the flyer (240) is corrected to 204. This example illustrates how Listening questions often include a deliberate correction of an earlier-mentioned distractor, and how numbers that sound similar (204 and 240) are a common source of careless errors.

Sample 5 — Sentence completion (Reading style). Passage extract: "Researchers found that participants who took short breaks every 90 minutes reported significantly higher levels of sustained attention throughout the working day compared with those who worked without scheduled breaks." Question: "Participants who took breaks every ______ reported higher levels of sustained attention." The correct answer is "90 minutes," taken directly from the passage. This type of question rewards careful scanning for the specific number or phrase that fits grammatically and logically into the gap, and candidates should check that their answer matches any word-limit instructions given for the task.

These samples highlight a recurring theme across both Listening and Reading: many questions hinge on small but critical details — a corrected number, the difference between "twice" and "doubled," or the distinction between a paragraph's main idea and its surrounding context. Careful, literal attention to the text or recording, combined with awareness of common distractor patterns, is one of the most effective ways to improve accuracy on these question types.
