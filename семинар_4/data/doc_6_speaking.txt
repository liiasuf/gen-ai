IELTS Academic: The Speaking Section

The Speaking test is identical for both the Academic and General Training versions of IELTS. It is conducted as a one-on-one interview between the candidate and a certified IELTS examiner, either in person or, at some test centers, over a video call. The entire Speaking test lasts between 11 and 14 minutes and is recorded for quality-assurance and remarking purposes. The Speaking test may be taken on the same day as the Listening, Reading, and Writing sections, or it may be scheduled separately, up to seven days before or after the other sections, depending on the test center's arrangements.

The Speaking test is divided into three parts. Part 1 lasts 4 to 5 minutes and consists of general questions about the candidate and a range of familiar topics, such as home, family, work, studies, and interests. The examiner introduces themselves, checks the candidate's identity, and then asks straightforward questions designed to help the candidate settle into the test.

Part 2 is often called the "long turn." The examiner gives the candidate a task card with a topic and several bullet points to address, along with a pencil and paper for making notes. The candidate has one minute to prepare and is then expected to speak on the topic for one to two minutes without further interruption from the examiner. After the candidate finishes, the examiner may ask one or two brief follow-up questions related to the same topic.

Part 3 lasts 4 to 5 minutes and consists of further questions related to the topic of Part 2, but at a more abstract and analytical level. The examiner and candidate engage in a discussion that explores ideas, opinions, and issues in greater depth, often involving comparisons, speculation about the future, evaluation of advantages and disadvantages, or discussion of broader societal implications connected to the Part 2 topic.

The Speaking test is assessed against four criteria: Fluency and Coherence (the ability to speak at a natural pace without excessive hesitation, and to organize ideas logically with appropriate linking), Lexical Resource (the range and accuracy of vocabulary, including the ability to discuss a variety of topics and to paraphrase when the right word is not immediately available), Grammatical Range and Accuracy (the range of grammatical structures used and how accurately they are produced), and Pronunciation (the ability to produce understandable speech with appropriate stress, rhythm, and intonation).

A common piece of advice for Part 1 is to give answers that are more than just one or two words, extending responses with brief explanations or examples, while still keeping the conversational, informal tone appropriate for this part of the test. For Part 2, effective use of the one-minute preparation time — for example, jotting down a few key words or a simple structure for each bullet point — can help candidates speak more fluently and stay on topic for the full one to two minutes without long pauses.

For Part 3, candidates benefit from practicing how to discuss abstract topics, give reasoned opinions, and use language for speculation and hypothesis, such as "It's likely that...," "One possible explanation could be...," or "If that were to happen, then...". Since this part of the test often touches on social, environmental, technological, or educational issues, building familiarity with vocabulary related to these broad topic areas — the same kind of academic vocabulary useful for Reading and Writing — can help candidates respond more confidently and fluently.

Unlike the Writing and Reading sections, the Speaking test does not reward memorized or rehearsed answers; examiners are trained to recognize responses that sound prepared in advance and may probe further with unexpected follow-up questions. The most effective preparation focuses on building general fluency, a wide topic-based vocabulary, and confidence speaking spontaneously, rather than memorizing scripts for specific topics.
